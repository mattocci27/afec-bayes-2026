Alpha and beta are the unknown coefficients. Alpha is log-odds at reference density x=0; beta changes log-odds per one SD of density. Neither coefficient is itself a probability or a percentage reduction. Here Normal(0,2.5) uses the SD as its second argument, as throughout the course. The two independent priors are unchanged and match stan/logit_reg.stan and the stored fit, whose normal(0,2.5) syntax uses an SD. Do not square the Stan SD. Read left to right: the priors describe uncertainty before seeing data; the observed data update them to the joint posterior for alpha and beta. Data enter the update through the likelihood from the preceding model slide. Independence is a prior assumption, not a claim that the posterior coefficients are independent. Day 1 calculated a posterior over a grid of p values; today we approximate the posterior for pairs using simulated draws. Do not imply simulation finds true parameters or that a grid is impossible for this two-parameter model. After motivating the prior scales on the next slide, introduce the sampling idea.

Prior scales matter

Points: observed proportions.
Curves: illustrative, not fitted.

\begin{aligned} \text{Very diffuse:}\quad &\beta\sim\mathcal N(0,10^4)\\[6pt] \text{Weakly informative:}\quad &\beta\sim\mathcal N(0,2.5) \end{aligned}

We use simulation to approximate the posterior.

The right figure compares prior shapes, before using observed outcomes. Normal(0,10^4) means SD 10000 and variance 10^8. Normal(0,2.5) means SD 2.5, matching the unchanged fitted-model slope prior. The horizontal range [-120,20] is deliberately zoomed in: most of the diffuse prior lies outside this window. These are relative densities f(beta)/f(0), not probability densities that integrate to one over the shown range. At beta=-100 the diffuse relative density is exp(-0.00005), about 0.99995; the weak relative density is exp(-800), positive but below plotting precision. Thus the diffuse prior gives equal-width neighbourhoods near -100 and 0 almost the same prior weight; the weak prior greatly downweights -100. Do not compare absolute density heights across priors from this peak-normalised plot. The diffuse Normal is proper and is not literally a flat prior; it illustrates locally almost flat weighting across the slopes being discussed. Keep that separate from the binomial likelihood evidence in the left observations.

Allow about 2 minutes, following the model and prior/posterior recap. These are illustrative curves, not fits. Keep alpha=logit(0.3), x in [-3,3] SD units, the binomial observation model and the link fixed. The green curve is exactly slide 7’s alpha and beta. The dashed orange curve changes only beta to -100. At x=0 both curves have p=0.3. Negative scaled density means below-average density, not a negative number of neighbours. The large logit slope becomes a near-step on the probability scale: the 99%-to-1% transition spans (qlogis(.99)-qlogis(.01))/100 = 0.0919024 SD of density. It is the abrupt transition across such a small density change that we judge implausible for this teaching setting, not merely that p approaches 0 or 1. Some ecological thresholds may be sharp; this is a contextual judgment, not a universal prohibition on steep slopes or a conclusion estimated from these data. Ask the before-data question first: would such an almost all-or-none change across a tiny density difference be ecologically credible in this setting? Then use the grey observations to show the additional evidence from the data.

The points reuse ndd=make_ndd_data() from the preceding data slide: all 60 simulated quadrats, with observed survival proportions suv/n. Point AREA is proportional to n (5-20 seedlings); larger samples give more precise proportions at a fixed underlying p. No jitter, filtering or new data were introduced. Individual proportions fluctuate under the binomial model; points need not lie on a probability curve. The two curves are not posterior estimates or fitted alternatives. The green curve reuses the illustrative generating values; do not claim its visual agreement is independent validation of the model.

For these observations the extreme curve also receives a much smaller binomial likelihood: it predicts near-certain survival for many quadrats with deaths and near-zero survival for many quadrats with survivors. These are count likelihoods, not unweighted distances from the plotted proportions. The likelihood and prior make distinct contributions. The observed mismatch is evidence from these data; it is not the justification for choosing a prior after looking at the same data. A flat prior leaves the likelihood to do the penalizing here. Weakly informative priors can express the separate ecological judgment before observing outcomes, which is especially useful when data are sparse. The preceding slide specifies the full model’s independent coefficient priors. Next explain how simulation approximates the posterior.

Flat on beta means constant prior density: equal-width intervals around -0.8 and -100 receive the same weight, holding alpha fixed. It does not mean that survival probabilities are uniform. Do not confuse density with a point probability. A flat prior on the entire real line is improper, but that is not the teaching argument here. Bounded flat priors can be proper, and flat priors can be appropriate in some settings. The point is that flatness does not automatically encode sensible predictions. Next ask how to retain uncertainty while giving extreme effects less prior weight.

Approximate the posterior using simulation

MCMC generates a sequence of parameter values from the posterior distribution.

The sampled values describe uncertainty about the parameter.

About two minutes. Begin with the purpose: we use simulation to obtain draws that represent the posterior and its uncertainty. MCMC is the method used here to generate those draws. MCMC means Markov chain Monte Carlo. Ask participants to follow one coloured chain: a recorded step becomes one draw. A rejected proposal keeps the current value, so repeated values must be counted too. The two starting values are 0.2 and 0.8. Do not derive the acceptance probability or compare MCMC algorithms here. With the symmetric proposals used in this illustration, moves to higher target density are accepted; some moves to lower density are accepted as well. This helps explore a distribution rather than only climbing to a peak.

These are the existing main.qmd teaching animations, generated by R/mh.R with seed 123, 200 steps per chain and symmetric proposals of +/-0.01. Their target is proportional to p^110 * (1-p)^90, corresponding to a Beta(111,91) density; it is a separate probability illustration, not today’s CNDD fit or Day 1’s data. The proposal grid gives a discrete approximation to that continuous target. The right GIF’s axis says ‘Posterior’, but its stored values are LOG unnormalised density (110log(p)+90log(1-p)); explain this distinction orally. It is not a histogram, a normalised posterior density or a plot of visit counts. Explain that a histogram of many adequately explored retained values would approximate the distribution. Neither this short animation nor overlapping chains proves adequate exploration. The grey initial segment illustrates initial movement; the actual CNDD plots that follow show post-warmup draws.
