Likelihood

Normal model: from parameters to data

\boxed{f(\text{data}\mid\text{parameters})} \qquad \text{e.g.}\quad\operatorname{Normal}(x_i\mid\mu,\sigma)

Probability density of the data given the parameters.

Three observations: x=\{-1.5,\;0,\;1.5\}.

  • How high is the curve at each observed value?
  • For continuous data, this height is a probability density.
  • The original Normal examples and data come from main.qmd at commit 3013b7c.
  • The vertical bar means “given”: evaluate the data under specified parameter values.
  • f is a probability density function; here we choose the Normal density. The expression on the right evaluates it at one observation x_i, with sigma denoting the standard deviation.
  • The marks on the horizontal axis show the observed data; the dots on the curve show their densities.
  • Assume the three observations are independent and follow the same Normal model.
  • Throughout the course, Normal(mu, sigma) uses the standard deviation as its second argument, as in Stan and dnorm().
  • Density is not the probability of observing an exact value; for continuous data, probabilities are areas over intervals.
  • The displayed notation f(data | parameters) means the joint density; this plot shows each observation’s density before combining them on the next slide.

Normal model: from data to likelihood

The observations x=\{-1.5,0,1.5\} and \sigma=1 are fixed.

  • Different values of \mu give different probability densities at the observed values.
  • The product of these densities depends on \mu.
  • This product, viewed as a function of \mu, is the likelihood L(\mu).

L(\mu)=f(-1.5\mid\mu,1)\,f(0\mid\mu,1)\,f(1.5\mid\mu,1)

Which fits better: \mu=0 or \mu=2?

  • This graph compares means 0 and 2, with SD fixed at 1; the observed x values stay in the same places.
  • Each candidate mean gives three density heights and one product. Changing mu changes the heights and their product; the mapping from mu to that product is the likelihood function L(mu).
  • Say: “The observations stay fixed. We change the mean, read the three heights, and multiply them. Each mean gives a different product. We call this product, as a function of the mean, the likelihood.”
  • Allow about 30 seconds for individual prediction; invite a brief explanation before the R comparison.
  • The prediction, R comparison and likelihood-curve explanation use about 3 minutes of the existing 8-23 minute likelihood block, not an additional code exercise.
  • Multiplying the densities is justified by the independence assumption.

Compare the two means in R

Keep x=\{-1.5,\;0,\;1.5\} and \sigma=1 fixed. Change only the mean.

log=TRUE returns log densities. Their sum is the log-likelihood.

Mean = 0

dnorm(-1.5, mean = 0, sd = 1, log = TRUE) +
  dnorm(0, mean = 0, sd = 1, log = TRUE) +
  dnorm(1.5, mean = 0, sd = 1, log = TRUE)
  [1] -5.006816

Mean = 2

dnorm(-1.5, mean = 2, sd = 1, log = TRUE) +
  dnorm(0, mean = 2, sd = 1, log = TRUE) +
  dnorm(1.5, mean = 2, sd = 1, log = TRUE)
  [1] -11.00682

Which value is larger? Mean = 0 fits these data better.

Taking logs preserves the ordering: larger likelihood, larger log-likelihood.

  • Restore the explicit dnorm calculation from main.qmd, with short lines for readability.
  • Ask what stays fixed and which argument changes. Participants may read the code and explain their prediction; running it is optional.
  • Reveal the two values only after participants have explained their predictions: about -5.007 and -11.007.
  • These are log-likelihoods, not probabilities or posterior probabilities of the two means.
  • The dnorm terms are densities. Independence makes their product the joint likelihood; taking logs turns the product into a sum.
  • A larger log-likelihood is better here even though both values are negative. No log derivation, derivative or optimisation code is required.

Log-likelihood as a function of \mu

With the data and \sigma=1 fixed, plot \log L(\mu) as a function of the parameter \mu.

The log-likelihood is highest at \mu=0.

  • This curve appears after the prediction and numerical comparison, so it does not reveal the answer first.
  • The horizontal axis is now the parameter mu; the earlier Normal curves used the observed value x on the horizontal axis.
  • The vertical axis shows the sum of the three log densities, with no rescaling: the same log-likelihood calculated in the preceding R comparison.
  • Taking logs preserves the position of the maximum. The log-likelihood is a function of mu for fixed data, not a posterior probability density for mu.
  • Connect to the next slide: for seedling counts we compare p instead of the Normal mean, using a binomial likelihood.

Binomial MLE: back to the seedlings

We return to two survivors out of five.

  • Now compare survival probabilities p instead of Normal means \mu.
  • The maximum is at p=0.4.
  • This is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE).

L(p)=\operatorname{Binomial}(2\mid5,p)=\binom{5}{2}p^2(1-p)^3 \qquad \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac{2}{5}=0.4

  • The data stay fixed, just as in the Normal example; now the parameter is a survival probability.
  • Binomial(2 | 5, p) is the probability of observing 2 survivors out of 5 seedlings, given survival probability p. It is a probability mass, rather than a continuous density.
  • The trial count n = 5 is fixed and known; only p is inferred. The conditioning side can include known quantities as well as unknown parameters.
  • Say: “For each survival probability p, calculate the probability of observing two survivors out of five. That gives us the likelihood at that p.”
  • Assume five independent seedlings with the same survival probability p.
  • The MLE makes the observed two survivors most likely under this model.
  • The graph uses dbinom(), including the binomial coefficient, with no rescaling. At p = 0 and p = 1, the likelihood is zero.

Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

Conditional Probability

Probability of A occurring given B has already occurred:

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}

e.g.,

  • P(hangover) = 4%
  • P(baijiu) = 1.5%
  • P(hangover \cap baijiu) = 1.275%.
  • P(hangover | baijiu) = 85%.
  • probability A occurs given B
  • vertical bar = given
  • at least 1 day for each month
  • the next day after I had beer
  • the next day after I had baijiu

Bayes’ Theorem

P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}

P(A \mid B) \times P(B) = P(B \mid A) \times P(A)

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \times P(A)}{P(B)}

Why is this useful?

  • Read the first row from left to right: given beer, what is the chance of a hangover?
  • This is the forward direction: start with a cause or parameter and predict an outcome or data.
  • In a statistical model, the forward quantity is P(\mathrm{Data} \mid \mathrm{Parameter}).
  • Now reverse the conditioning: given a hangover, what is the chance that beer was involved?
  • This is the inverse direction: use observed data to learn about an unknown cause or parameter.
  • Bayes’ theorem relates this posterior to the forward probability and the prior.

Bayes’ theorem

\textcolor{#d95f02}{ P(\mathrm{Parameter} \mid \mathrm{Data}) } = \frac{ \textcolor{#1b9e77} { P(\mathrm{Data} \mid \mathrm{Parameter}) } \times \textcolor{#7570b3} { P(\mathrm{Parameter})} } {P(\mathrm{Data})}

  • \textcolor{#d95f02}{P(\mathrm{Parameter} \mid \mathrm{Data})}

    • What we want to estimate (posterior): When we got our data, what were the parameters behind the data (e.g., survival probability, coefficients of regressions)?

  • \textcolor{#1b9e77}{P(\mathrm{Data} \mid \mathrm{Parameter})}

    • When we know our parameters, what is the probability of getting our data? (i.e., likelihood)

  • \textcolor{#7570b3}{P(\mathrm{Parameter})}

    • What we assume about the parameter before we see the data (i.e., prior).

  • P(\mathrm{Data})

    • Does not depend on the parameters (i.e., a constant)

Prior and posterior

Seedlings again: posterior \propto prior × likelihood

Likelihood:
2 survivors out of 5
y \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(5,p)

Prior:
All values of p are equally plausible.
p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,1)

Posterior:
Posterior \propto Prior \times Likelihood
p\mid y \sim \operatorname{Beta}(3,4)

Posterior mean: 0.43

Peak: 0.4 (= MLE)

95% credible interval: [0.12, 0.78]

  • Beta(1,1) prior + 2 of 5 -> Beta(3, 4) posterior
  • The equal-tailed 95% credible interval uses the 2.5th and 97.5th posterior percentiles.
  • The pale orange region under the posterior contains 95% of its probability; the shading uses the unrounded quantiles, while the text rounds the endpoints to 0.12 and 0.78.
  • The likelihood is omitted from the graph because it has the same shape as the posterior under this uniform prior. The likelihood itself is not a probability density for p.
  • contrast the interval statement with a confidence interval
  • this small table (mean / interval / probability statement) is the reporting pattern for the whole course

Prior vs. Data: MLE and Bayesian Inference

A: 3 heads out of 5 tosses → MLE = 0.6
B: 60 heads out of 100 tosses → MLE = 0.6

  • Allow 3–5 minutes for these two slides, including a prediction before revealing the updates.
  • Ask: will each posterior peak be closer to 0.5 or to 0.6?
  • The observed proportion and prior are identical, so the comparison isolates the amount of data.
  • Both likelihoods use the full Binomial probability, divided by their own value at the MLE p = 0.6. Each peak is one; neither curve is normalised to have area one.
  • The left panel compares likelihood widths. The right panel shows a probability density for the single prior used for both data sets; its vertical scale is independent.
  • Dataset A is salmon and solid, dataset B is green and dashed, and the prior is purple and dotdash on both this slide and the following posterior comparison.
  • This deliberately strong prior illustrates its influence; it is not a default prior for ecological survival.

Bayesian updating: More data, less influence from the prior

Post A (5 tosses): The prior has a stronger influence, so the posterior stays close to the prior.

Post B (100 tosses): The data have more influence, shifting the posterior toward 0.6.

The posteriors are Beta(54,53) and Beta(111,91). MAPs are 53/105 and 110/200. The means differ from the peaks. At 100 tosses the prior still has substantial influence. These are deliberate teaching priors. Do not use them as defaults. No derivation is required. Explain the figure before discussing the numbers.

Summary of Day 1

  • Likelihood: how probable are the data for given parameter values.
  • Bayes’ theorem turns this around: posterior \propto likelihood \times prior.
  • The posterior is a distribution. Its location describes plausible values; its width describes uncertainty.
  • The influence of a fixed prior depends on the information in the data.
